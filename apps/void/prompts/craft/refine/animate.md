# Refine: animate

Motion in a video points the eye, shows a relationship or a change, and gives the piece its voice. Decoration
without a purpose is motion debt: it costs attention the viewer needed for the words.

## Find the jobs
Animate only where motion will:
- bring something in on the word that says it, or take it out to make room;
- show a change or a relationship (a bar filling to its true value, a before becoming an after, a number counting
  from its real start);
- direct attention to the moment that matters;
- carry the direction's signature move.
Don't animate a part just because it exists.

## Write a motion thesis
Before keying anything, decide in a few lines:
- **the focal moment**: the one sequence that deserves authorship, from this piece's idea (a generic fade-and-rise
  isn't a thesis);
- **continuity**: what must read as the same thing moving rather than a cut between two things;
- **the grammar**: how parts enter and leave in this piece, one family, varied by importance.

## Timing (at 30 fps)
| Frames | Use |
|---|---|
| 3-5 | a hit, a highlight lighting, feedback |
| 5-9 | a routine entrance or exit |
| 9-15 | a part that moves across the frame, a reveal |
| 15-24 | the authored focal moment |
Exits are faster than entrances. Ease out (fast start, gentle arrival) for things arriving; ease in for things
leaving. Bounce and elastic only when the direction calls for play, never by reflex.

## Material by meaning
Position and opacity are the base. Scale for emphasis and punch-ins (slow, small: a clip's zoom over a line, not a
jump). Masks, wipes and crops for reveals when the renderer can draw them. A stagger only for things that really
are a list, with the total delay capped.

## Verify
The focal moment is specific to this piece; every other move explains something; nothing moves the eye away from
the words while they're being read; removing any animation would lose meaning, not just motion. Look at stills of
entrance frames; the TSX holds the exact values.
